Steel Colors

Steel tells you how hot it is. Twice. Once as it glows in the fire. Again as oxide colors creep across a bright surface when you temper. Learn to read both.

Colors are a guide. Not a thermometer. What you see changes with the steel, the light, the scale on the surface and your own eyes. Every number here is rough. Sources disagree by 50°F on temper colors and 100°F or more on heat colors. Use a magnet, test files and a real thermometer when it matters.

2. Temper colors

Harden a tool and it's glass-hard and brittle. Tempering gives back toughness. Polish a spot bright, heat it gently and an oxide film grows. Thicker film, different color. The color tells you roughly how far you've drawn it.

    3. Draw a temper from the back

    Old trick. No oven needed. You heat the thick part and let the heat run down to the edge.

    1. Harden the tool. Polish a strip bright so you can see color.
    2. Heat the back or the thick part. Not the edge.
    3. Watch the colors run toward the edge. Straw first, then bronze, purple, blue.
    4. When the edge hits the color you want, quench it. Done.

    Why the colors lie a little

    Bright light washes out glow. Smiths used to heat treat in dark corners for a reason. Outside in the sun a low red can look like plain black steel and still burn you.

    Scale and flux change the look. So does the steel. A high alloy steel throws different temper colors than plain carbon steel. Time matters too. Hold steel hot longer and the oxide keeps growing, so you get a deeper color at a lower heat.

    For hardening, a magnet beats your eye. Carbon steel goes non-magnetic around 1,414°F. Heat just past that and quench. For tempering, an oven and a test file beat colors. Colors are still fast, free and good enough for a lot of shop tools.
