The only equation that matters

Force = pressure × piston area
Area = π × (5 ÷ 2)² = 19.63 in²
19.63 in² × 3000 psi = 58,900 lb ≈ 29.4 tons
Change the bore or the relief setting above and the numbers follow. Bigger bore means more tons at the same pressure. More pressure means more tons from the same cylinder. The pump does not make force. The cylinder does.

Why the ram slows down on steel

Stage 1: 11 gpm ÷ 19.63 in² = 2.2 in/s
Stage 2: 2.5 gpm ÷ 19.63 in² = 0.5 in/s
A two-stage pump is two pumps on one shaft. The big one moves a lot of oil at low pressure so the ram closes fast. The moment the ram meets steel and pressure climbs past the unloading point the big pump dumps to tank and only the small one keeps pushing. Less oil per second means a slower ram but it can push to full pressure without stalling the motor.

Why smiths like a press

It moves the middle. A hammer blow is fast and the energy dies near the surface on thick stock. A press squeezes slowly and the whole cross section flows. That is why a press is the tool for drawing down big billets and for forge welding stacks.

It is controllable. You can creep the ram down a sixteenth at a time with the handle. Tooling like fullers, drifts and spring swages sits still on the die instead of bouncing.

It is quiet. No ring. Neighbors do not hear it. Your ears thank you.

It stalls on wide stock. Force is fixed. As the bar spreads the contact area grows and the pressure needed climbs until it hits the relief setting. Watch the gauge peg and the ram stop. The fix is smaller dies or a smaller bite, not a bigger motor.

Where the sim cheats. The bar is a row of 56 slices and each slice only gets thinner, wider or longer in straight lines. Die friction and the extra squeeze it takes to spread a wide thin section are folded into one factor on the flow stress. There is no bulging of the bar sides and no steel moving between slices except by stretching. Good enough for the idea.
